Guide

How to store DTF transfers

Finished DTF transfers keep best sealed, flat, dry and at room temperature. Damp is the main enemy. Fold one through the print, or leave a stack in a hot car, and they stick to each other or curl before you press them.

These are starting points for ready-made DTF transfers. Always follow the instructions supplied with your transfers, then test on a scrap. The number on a heat press dial is often not the true platen temperature.

The short version

  • Sealed. Sabur Digital recommend sealed bags, any zip-lock will do, because moisture is what spoils transfers before you use them.
  • Dry and at room temperature. DTF Custom Transfers ask for a dry place at a steady room temperature, away from extreme heat or cold.
  • Out of sunlight. Heat through a window can affect the adhesive.
  • Flat, not folded. They say lay transfers flat, not upright or at an angle, so they do not curl.
  • Small stacks. They suggest no more than 50–100 transfers in a stack so they do not stick together.
  • Cut gang sheets up. Separate pieces are easier to store and to find.

What UK suppliers publish

Storage advice from supplier pages, checked 11 November 2026
SupplierShelf life they giveHow they say to store
DTF Custom TransfersWithin six months to a year for best resultsDry, room temperature, out of sunlight, airtight container, flat, stacks of 50–100 at most
Sabur DigitalNot statedSealed bags, kept absolutely dry

That is two suppliers, not a law of DTF. Films, inks and powders differ. If your supplier prints a date or a storage note, that is the instruction.

Handling the stack

Keep transfers flat, in a sleeve or between sheets of paper, out of direct sun. A cool cupboard beats a windowsill and beats the back of a van in July. Damp air can leave the film tacky. If a sheet feels wet or the powder looks disturbed, do not press a customer’s shirt with it. Press a scrap, or discard it.

Do not fold through the design. A crease in the adhesive shows up as a line on the garment. Cut gang-sheet pieces want the same treatment. Write the peel type and the date on the sleeve if you have more than one film in the drawer. Cold, warm and hot are easy to mix up a month later, and the peel is how prints get ruined in the first second after the press opens.

If an older batch starts lifting at the edges, work through not sticking before blaming the press. Pre-press the garment as usual. A transfer that has taken on damp is not rescued by a hotter press.

Common questions

How long do DTF transfers last before pressing?

Go by your supplier. DTF Custom Transfers recommend using theirs within six months to a year, and say storage conditions change that. Use older sheets first, and if a batch is past the time your supplier gave, press a test piece before you promise an order.

How do I know an old transfer is still good?

Look before you press. DTF Custom Transfers say transfers that are hard to peel, do not stick after pressing, or look wrinkled have probably expired, and suggest testing a small sample first. A test on a scrap costs one transfer, not a customer's shirt.

The film has stuck to the next one.

Warmth or pressure did that. Peel them apart slowly. If the adhesive has lifted off its own film, that piece is done. Do not press it and hope the shirt will sort it out.

Can I keep them in the garage or the loft?

Only if it stays dry and does not get hot. Both suppliers cited here put moisture first, and DTF Custom Transfers ask for a steady room temperature away from extremes. Unheated outbuildings and lofts swing through damp and heat across a year, so a cupboard in the house is the safer bet.

Sources
